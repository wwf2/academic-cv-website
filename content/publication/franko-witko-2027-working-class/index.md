abstract: Why do so many Americans identify as working class and how does working
  class identity matter in American politics? This book argues that subjective working
  class identity, distinct from objective indicators like income, education, or occupation,
  is a consequential and underexamined force in U.S. politics and policy. Drawing
  on social identity theory, Franko and Witko show that identification with the working
  class shapes how people interpret their economic interests and navigate political
  choices, independent of their objective class position. par Against the conventional
  wisdom that America is uniquely a \"middle class society,\" and against claims that
  class has become obsolete in a post-industrial economy, the book demonstrates that
  working class identification remains widespread and politically meaningful. It traces
  how this identity varies across time and demographic groups, how it is shaped by
  broad economic transformations like rising inequality, stagnant mobility, and economic
  insecurity, and how it is actively constructed by political actors such as parties
  and unions. The book further shows that some economically secure, highly educated
  professionals paradoxically identify as working class, while some in objectively
  working class positions identify upward, complicating simple materialist accounts.
  par Situating class identity alongside racial, ethnic, gender, and religious identities,
  the authors show that working class identity exerts independent effects on policy
  attitudes, voting behavior, and broader political outcomes. The book offers a contemporary
  account of class politics in the United States.
